How many sensors does a plant need?

A digital twin of two water tanks: one physical sensor, a virtual one with honest error bars, overflow warnings, and a model that tells the crew which part is wearing out.

A physics-based model of two cascaded tanks forecasts the water level from the pump signal alone, within 2 % of full scale, and states how sure it is. It replaces a second sensor for forecasting. It also turns sensor data into plain findings such as "lower outlet partly clogged", which no black-box model can give.

The design question

Picture two reservoirs in cascade. A pump fills the upper one, it drains into the lower one, and the lower one drains back to a supply tank. Food and beverage plants, water utilities and chemical lines are full of setups like this: buffer tanks, mixing vessels, settling basins. Two things must never happen. A tank must not overflow, because spilled product means loss, cleaning and sometimes fines. The pump must not run dry, because that damages it.

So the engineer has to decide: where do the sensors go, and how many do we buy? A level sensor on every tank costs money to buy, wire, calibrate and maintain. Fewer sensors means part of the plant is invisible.

One sensor and a virtual one

This case study uses a public benchmark: a real two-tank rig with one capacitive level sensor, on the lower tank only. The upper tank is never measured. The model starts from physics everyone learns in school: water flows out of a hole at a speed set by the water height above it (Torricelli's law), what goes in must come out, and a full tank overflows. To that it adds a few effects the data revealed, such as the lower tank overflowing just above the point where the sensor saturates.

From the pump signal and the one sensor, the model keeps a running estimate of both water levels, including the unmeasured one. It is a virtual sensor, and like a real sensor it comes with an error bar.

How good is it?

On the benchmark's test record, the model forecasts the lower-tank level from the pump signal alone, with no level measurements after the first few minutes. Its typical error is 0.198 V on a 10 V scale. Its 95 % range contains the real level 93.5 % of the time, and 98.7 % of the time during overflow, which is when it matters most.

The best published result is 0.007 V better, a difference smaller than the sensor's own noise. The deep networks trained on the same 1024 samples are 50 to 100 % worse, and their errors pile up in the rare overflow episodes, where they matter most.

Is a second sensor worth buying?

With the model in hand, the question can be answered before buying anything. Using the fitted model as a simulated plant, we compared one sensor on the lower tank, as built, with a second one added on the upper tank.

  • For forecasting, the second sensor barely helps. The uncertainty about the upper level shrinks from about ±0.08 V to ±0.06 V, and overflow warnings at least a minute ahead go from 51 % to 55 % of episodes, with the same false-alarm rate.
  • For maintenance, it pays off. Pump wear is found in 2.4 minutes instead of 32, and a clog in the upper outlet becomes detectable at all; with one sensor it was missed in every simulated run.

So the answer depends on what the plant needs: one sensor and the virtual one if the goal is to avoid overflow, two if the goal is also to catch wear early. The model puts a number on that choice. This part is a simulation study with the fitted model, not a measurement on the real rig.

Findings the crew can act on

Every number in the model is a physical quantity: how fast each outlet drains, how much the pump delivers per volt, where each tank overflows. When the plant changes, the model's numbers change with it, and that change has a meaning. Instead of "the error went up", the operator reads "lower-tank outlet flow is 18 % below normal: likely a partial clog". In the simulated plant, no such finding appeared on a healthy plant.

The model also protects itself from bad data. Each reading gets an inferred trust weight. A glitch from a faulty sensor gets a low weight and is set aside, so one bad value cannot drive a wrong action.

What deployment looks like

  • On the premises. The model takes a fraction of a millisecond per update on a laptop; the rig samples every 4 seconds. It fits a PLC, an industrial PC or a Raspberry Pi, with no cloud connection and no process data leaving the site.
  • Little data per site. A new installation needs its handful of physical constants refitted from a short recording, not months of training data.
  • Behaviour you can check. The assumptions are written down, the uncertainty is reported, and the code reproduces the model to twelve digits.

What it does not do

The best published black-box correction is slightly more accurate on average, by less than the sensor noise. The value of a second sensor and the fault diagnosis were studied on the fitted model as a simulated plant, not on the physical rig. And the benchmark covers one rig and one operating range; a new plant needs its own short calibration.

Technical details

The model is a grey-box state-space model with Torricelli outflow, overflow at both tanks, a sensor that saturates at 10 V, and a small learned correction, inferred with tempered message passing. A paper describing it is in preparation for IFAC MICNON 2027.
